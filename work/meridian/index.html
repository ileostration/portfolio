01The problem( Intent and research )

01Zone 01 · 87°W · 14.07°N

Biennial identitiesillustrate a word.

Most biennials invent a metaphor for an abstract theme, then lose visitors between buildings, and the catalogue, signs and website come from three vendors that do not match. This brief asked for one system that organises a visit across seven venues.

( Sources )

What I read

Kevin Lynch, The Image of the City: paths, edges, districts, nodes, landmarks.

Colour as index (Peirce): a colour points at a thing without describing it.

Gestalt continuity: the eye follows a line through a gap.

Systematic identity in the tradition of Massimo Vignelli’s transit graphics.

The Swiss poster: a grid, a few sizes of type, one strong element.

02The device( Intent )

02Zone 02 · 74°W · 40.71°N

A map is a systemeveryone reads.

A map is a graphic system everyone already knows how to read. Give the exhibition the order of a map and the design stops needing to explain itself.

Each venue was assigned a real meridian. Curators asked artists to respond to what crosses it: a border, a river, a migration route. The line stopped being a theme and became a route. Fourteen cities sit on those seven lines.

04The mark( Branding )

03Zone 03 · 2°E · 48.86°N

Three directions,one mark.

The mark had to read like a map, not an art-world logo: precise, public, legible from across a street.

Seven ellipses in one circle read as a globe before they read as a logo. Each meridian can take a zone colour, so the mark can say which venue you are at.

Stacked numerals match the numbers on the walls, the map and the ticket. It is clear, but it reads as a list and cannot become a line on a floor.

One vertical cut is the simplest mark that carries the whole idea. On its own it is a wordmark, not a place, so it needs a symbol beside it.

EssayIlse Vandermeer

Seven lines

A biennial is usually asked to say something about the world, and the buildings that host it are left to say nothing. This one began with the opposite question: what does a visitor need in order to find the next room?

We started with a map. Chicago sits on a sphere, and so does everything that crosses it. Seven meridians were chosen, from 87 degrees west to 151 degrees east, and each one was given to a venue. Curators asked the artists to answer to what their line crosses: a border, a river, a migration route, a city that shares a longitude with another and little else.

8

EssaySeven lines

The order is west to east. The first line runs through Pilsen, where the biennial opens on 6 March; the last ends in the Loop, where it closes on 20 June. Between them a visitor crosses seven lines and fourteen cities, and never has to ask which way to go.

Nothing in this book explains the idea, because nothing on the walls does. The same numbers run down the gutter, along the floor and across the ticket. If you can follow the line in this book, you can follow it through the city.

Ilse Vandermeer
Artistic Director, Meridian Foundation
